Most B2B marketing strategies look fine on a slide and fall apart by month three. I've spent more than 13 years agency-side and sat in plenty of reviews where nobody could say what marketing had actually delivered. So here's the short answer: a good **b2b marketing strategy framework** starts with the business goal, maps to how your buyers actually buy, puts effort only where revenue is likely, and reports on pipeline rather than traffic. Everything else is detail.

Below is the framework I use with clients, and the places where I see it break in practice.

## Start with the business goal, not the tactic

The most common mistake I see is starting backwards. Someone decides "we need SEO" or "we need more LinkedIn" before anyone has written down what the business is trying to achieve.

SEO is a tactic. So is paid search, email, events and content. None of them is a business goal. Before any b2b marketing plan gets written, I want three things on paper:

- **The business goal.** One plain statement, such as entering a new sector, growing a particular service line, or reducing reliance on a handful of big accounts.
- **The marketing goal that supports it.** Is the job to build awareness, grow top-of-funnel interest, or attract buyers who are ready to talk now? These need very different plans.
- **The tactics.** Only now do you choose channels, because you know what they're for.

If you can't fill in the first line, stop. Nothing further down the page will fix it.

## Map the plan to how buyers actually buy

I use a simple model called AC/DC to keep plans grounded in the buyer's reality:

- **Awareness:** they've spotted a problem, or are only just realising they have one, and start looking for answers.
- **Consideration:** they're working out what kind of solution they need and which providers might help.
- **Decision:** they've evaluated their options and are ready to commit.
- **Customer:** they've bought, and with luck they become an advocate as well.

Each stage needs different content and different actions. A thought-leadership article won't close a deal, and a pricing page won't create awareness. When a b2b marketing strategy feels busy but unproductive, it's usually because everything is aimed at one stage, often awareness, while the decision stage is neglected.

## The b2b marketing strategy framework I use: five stages

This grew out of my SEO work, but the logic applies across channels. Each stage has a clear deliverable, which makes it easy to explain to a board.

**1. Demand discovery: find the real buying intent.**
Research what your buyers are actively looking for, look at where competitors are filling gaps you aren't, and ignore anything that only brings vanity traffic. *Deliverable:* opportunities grouped by intent: researching, considering, ready to buy.

**2. Asset creation: build what buyers actually need.**
That usually means proper service and landing pages with clear calls to action and buying pathways, not just another blog post. Design for conversion as well as visibility. *Deliverable:* a page plan, then the pages themselves.

**3. Authority amplification: put trust where it matters.**
Focus link building, internal linking, PR and partnerships on the handful of pages that drive revenue, not everywhere at once. *Deliverable:* a realistic authority plan that targets only those priority pages.

**4. Performance optimisation: make buying easier.**
Watch how people behave on key pages, test calls to action, forms and headings, and remove friction. *Deliverable:* conversion insights and an action plan.

**5. Revenue reporting: track what the board cares about.**
Tie activity back to leads, pipeline and revenue in a simple dashboard. *Deliverable:* "this many qualified leads, this much pipeline, from this activity."

Everything in that chain points at one outcome: revenue.

## Where most B2B strategies fall apart

### Reporting on the wrong things

"Increase organic traffic by 20%" isn't a goal a sales director cares about. Compare it with "drive 30 sales-qualified leads from organic search in Q3." The second is something sales can hold you to, and something you can plan backwards from.

In practice, that means using your CRM or an attribution tool to connect marketing activity to closed revenue, tagging content by funnel stage, and reporting on "opportunities influenced" rather than rankings and impressions.

### Marketing and sales speak different languages

If marketing calls something a lead and sales calls it noise, the strategy is already in trouble. Three things help:

- **Interview the sales team.** Ask what objections and questions prospects raise, then build content that answers them, such as comparison pages and objection-handling pages.
- **Hold a monthly joint review.** Look at which pages and campaigns produced conversations that went somewhere, and adjust the plan.
- **Rename things if you need to.** Something like "intent-driven leads" can bridge the gap better than an acronym sales has learned to ignore.

### The approval bottleneck

This one rarely makes it into strategy decks, but it quietly kills momentum. Clients, or internal stakeholders, quite rightly want sign-off on what goes out. Then feedback stalls and months pass with nothing published.

That's a process design problem, and it's fixable:

- **Tiered content.** Brand-sensitive or product-specific material always needs approval. Evergreen, non-brand content within pre-agreed topics can go live if there's no response within an agreed window.
- **A quarterly content bank.** Agree a set of low-risk outlines up front so there's always something approved and ready.
- **An opt-out window.** "If we haven't heard back within seven working days, we'll proceed." Stakeholders keep the power to intervene, but there's a clear deadline.
- **Easier approvals.** Preview links and shared planning tools mean people can sign off in one click.

### Repeating what you've always done

I've watched businesses stick with a single channel long after the warning signs appeared, because changing felt risky. In my experience the bigger risk is doing the same thing again and expecting a different result. Diversifying and testing is risk management.

The same goes for untargeted, "spray and pray" outreach. If someone tells me the messaging is the problem, my questions are simple: what have you tested, who did you test it on, and can I see the results? A strategy without evidence is just an opinion.

### Reports that only look backwards

Monthly reports that recap last month don't earn a seat at the table. The more useful conversation brings cost and performance data across all channels into one ROI view, then uses it to frame what comes next: future growth, not past activity. That shift is what turns marketing from a supplier into a strategic partner.

## The boardroom takeaway

If you take one thing into your next board meeting, make it these five questions on a single page:

1. What is the business goal, in one sentence?
2. Which marketing goal supports it: awareness, interest or ready-to-buy demand?
3. Which buyer stage are we weakest at?
4. Where exactly will we focus effort, and what are we choosing *not* to do?
5. How will we show pipeline and revenue, not activity?

If your current b2b marketing plan can't answer all five, that's where to start.

## Final thought

None of this is complicated. The hard part is discipline: starting with the goal, saying no to vanity work, and fixing the unglamorous process problems that stall delivery. If you'd like a second pair of eyes on your current strategy, I'm always happy to talk it through.

## FAQ

**What is a B2B marketing strategy framework?**
It's a structured way of connecting business goals to marketing goals, buyer stages and tactics, with clear deliverables and reporting tied to pipeline and revenue.

**How is a b2b marketing plan different from a strategy?**
The strategy sets out what you're trying to achieve and why. The plan sets out the specific tactics, timings and owners that deliver it.

**Which metrics should B2B marketing report on?**
Focus on sales-qualified leads, opportunities influenced, meetings booked and revenue. Traffic and rankings only matter where they clearly link to those outcomes.
