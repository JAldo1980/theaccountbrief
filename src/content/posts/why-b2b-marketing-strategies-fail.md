Your traffic is up. Your impressions are up. Your pipeline isn't. Sound familiar?

Most B2B marketing strategies don't fail because the ideas are bad. They fail because the wrong things are being measured, the link to revenue is never properly made, and the plan on paper doesn't match the time and resource available to deliver it.

In this post I'll cover three areas where I see this go wrong again and again: confusing activity with growth, failing to connect marketing to pipeline and revenue, and the gap between planning and execution.

## Activity, impressions and traffic are not business growth

Let's be clear: activity, impressions and traffic matter. From an SEO point of view, they're useful signals. But they are not business goals, and in my experience they often have surprisingly little correlation with them.

The problem starts when teams turn activity into a proxy for results. You can have as much traffic and as many impressions as you like, but the real question is simple: are those numbers moving the thing the business actually wants to achieve? More often than not, the honest answer is no.

When a business has a website, it usually wants it to do one of two things. Either it drives an enquiry that becomes a marketing qualified lead (MQL) or sales qualified lead (SQL), or it sells something directly, as with e-commerce. Everything else is supporting detail.

That's where we need to separate strategy from tactics. Strategy is the overarching mission: getting the business from A to B. Tactics map out how you get there. Traffic and impressions can tell you whether a specific tactic is working, but they aren't necessarily an indication that your strategy is working. And if your tactics are measuring the wrong things, you can look busy while going nowhere.

So I always start with two questions:

- What are we actually measuring?
- Why does it matter to the business objective?

Once you can answer those, you know what to measure, and the metrics you report on start to mean something.

## Connecting marketing objectives to pipeline, sales and revenue

Ultimately, marketing has to show a return on investment. There has to be an output, something the business can point to and say: that came from our marketing.

The best way to do this is to connect your marketing to your CRM or accounts system so you can start joining the dots. And this needs to happen early. There's no point pouring time and budget into a channel if you can't get a clear readout from it or attribute it to a commercial outcome.

I saw this first-hand with a client who told us they were getting more enquiries through their website forms. Our reports didn't show it. When we dug in, it turned out their previous agency had never set up form tracking properly. So the enquiries were real, but nobody could see where they came from or credit the work behind them.

We put the tracking in, and two things changed straight away. The client could finally see which channels were driving their enquiries, and we could attribute the results to the work we were doing. Same marketing, same leads, but now there was a clear line from effort to outcome.

Take a simple example: an enquiry form on your website. That form should be well optimised, easy to use and quick to complete. Keep it short, succinct and fast, with one single purpose, usually to get the prospect speaking to an expert who can move the enquiry forward.

But every step in that journey has a cost, and the pipeline should reflect it. An enquiry can be given a cost. An MQL can be given a cost. An SQL can be given a cost. And each stage should also carry a value. When you do this, the business can see two things clearly throughout the process:

- The potential commercial impact marketing is creating in the pipeline
- The actual revenue marketing has generated from specific enquiries and digital assets

That's the difference between reporting on marketing and proving its worth.

## Where strategy breaks down between planning and execution

Strategy can break down between planning and execution in lots of ways, but it usually comes back to time and resource.

You can have the best strategy in the world, but if the time and resource aren't calibrated correctly, delivery will fall apart. There's no point committing to a multi-channel brand positioning and messaging strategy if you don't have the internal capacity, or the budget for external help, to see it through.

You also need to know how you'll measure it, what you'll take from that measurement, and how it will affect the bottom line. A strategy can look perfectly aligned to business objectives on paper, but without the people, time and talent to deliver it, and a clear way to measure its return, it simply won't land.

A simple test before you sign off any strategy: for every channel or activity in the plan, ask three questions. Who is going to deliver it? How many hours a month will it realistically take? And how will we measure what it brings in? If you can't answer all three, that part of the plan isn't ready yet.

In short: your strategy must be realistic within your means, and it must be measurable. ROI is a term we use so often it can lose its weight, but without it, you're working hard on something that was never going to work.

## Key takeaways

- Measure what matters. Step away from vanity metrics and focus on the measures that reflect real commercial outcomes.
- Connect it to revenue. Make sure what you measure links to your strategic objectives and, ultimately, to pipeline and revenue.
- Be realistic about delivery. Match your strategy to the time, resource and talent you actually have, and make sure every part of it can be measured.

Marketing that can't prove its commercial value will always be the first budget line to be cut. Marketing that can will always earn its place at the table.
